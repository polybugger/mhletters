## 1. The story in one paragraph

Simla, October 1880. A respected newspaper editor has watched a Russian woman produce marvels at picnics and dinner parties, and he wants to prove them to the world. He proposes a test no skeptic could argue with. The reply comes from a man he has never met, an adept who signs himself Koot' Hoomi. It is a refusal, and a lesson. The more convincing the proof, the more dangerous it would be. Science, the letter says, has always punished what it couldn't yet explain. The crowd destroys the messengers it can reach. Truth must come "step by step, not at a rush". The editor ends the letter disappointed, but with orders: publish what you have already seen, and stand behind it with your name.

## 2. Approach

**The framing device.** The letter is a monologue, so the script stages it as a reading. Sinnett opens the letter at his desk in Simla. K.H.'s voice (V.O.) reads it to him, and to us. As the argument moves, the scene leaves the study for what the words describe: Hyde Park, the Royal Society, Hooke's study, a cave in the Himalaya, Galileo before the Inquisition. Each time it comes back to Sinnett, so we watch the letter land on him.

**K.H. on screen.** K.H. appears only as a presence. We see him in fragments or at a distance, or in the "vision" spaces the letter conjures, never in the same room as Sinnett. This keeps faith with the letter: he says he "listened attentively to the conversation which took place at Mr. Hume's" (L78), but he is never physically there.

**Neutral voice.** The script dramatizes what the letter *claims*. It doesn't endorse or mock the claims (see analysis §11). Phenomena are shown as the witnesses described them, and staged so the viewer can't tell how they happened.

## 3. Dialogue rules

These keep the script honest about what comes from the letter and what is invented.

1. **K.H.'s lines are the letter, word for word.** Every K.H. line is quoted exactly from `letter-001.md` and tagged with its line number. Lines may be cut, and split across shots, but never reworded or added to.
2. **Everyone else's dialogue is invented** and is marked **[dramatized]** in the scene files. It must agree with the letter and analysis. It may restate what the letter says the character said or did ("You say — half London would be converted…", L26) or what the analysis gives as context. It must not invent new historical facts.
3. **Historical figures in the vision sequences are mostly silent.** They act out what the letter says about them. They speak only words the letter itself quotes, such as Aristarchus's "affirmeth that the Earth moveth circularly about her own centre" (L63) and Recorde's colleagues' "vain phantasies" (L64).
4. **The letter's history is played as the letter tells it.** The script doesn't correct or annotate its historical claims, so the scenes stay focused on the letter. Any fact-check material (analysis §10) is kept out of the scenes. If wanted, it can go in text cards after the final scene.
5. **Context-only events** (the picnic, the brooch, Sinnett writing his proposal) come from the analysis, which flags their details as approximate. The script keeps them general and marks them **[context]**.

### Scene summaries

**01 — The Marvels of Simla** *(prologue, [context])*

- Early October 1880. On a hillside picnic above Simla, a missing cup and saucer is reportedly dug out of the ground. At dinner at Hume's, a lost brooch is said to have turned up. The guests marvel, and the skeptics smile.
- That night Sinnett paces his study, sure he has a story that could convert London if the proof were strong enough. He writes out his proposal, the newspaper test (analysis §4), and hands it to Blavatsky. She warns him that the Masters do not perform on demand.
- This scene sets up the world, the people and the stakes: Sinnett's reputation as an editor, and the ridicule already falling on Blavatsky.

**02 — Unthinkable** (L11–L27)

- About 15 October. The reply arrives. Sinnett opens it eagerly. K.H.'s voice begins: "Esteemed Brother and Friend, Precisely because the test of the London newspaper would close the mouths of the skeptics — it is unthinkable."
- His face falls. The voice explains: the world is "unprepared". Science can't explain the event, and the masses would call it a miracle or "dark agencies".
- Vision: the test succeeds, and a crowd in Hyde Park turns on Sinnett. "They would kill you before you could make the round of Hyde Park." If it fails, his "reputation and good name" are gone.
- Cut to Blavatsky as she was earlier, rushing "into the wide open door leading to notoriety". The door becomes "a trap — and a fatal one indeed for her".

**03 — Rome Before Romulus** (L29–L42)

- The letter turns to Sinnett's "race" and its boast of free thought. Allegory: prejudice and bigotry, "bottled up like the wicked *Jin* of old", sealed by "the Solomons of science" at the bottom of the sea. The bottle trembles.
- 1662: the Royal Society receives its charter, as the letter tells it, with Bacon, Boyle and the Bishop of Chester.
- "*Roma ante Romulum fuit*": behind Archimedes in Syracuse rise older ruins.
- The *vril* of "the Coming Race" glows. Bulwer-Lytton's book lies on Sinnett's desk.
- The camera enters the cave in the Himavats, "full of the skeletons of these giants". The blue light of *Akás* fills it. Science calls it "an impossibility, a myth".

**04 — The Modern Hookeses** (L43–L48)

- A short, wry scene. Hooke at his microscope, classifying surfaces. Newton's "fine discoveries found in him their greatest opponent."
- Back in the present, the men of science want only what is "*plausible*", not what is "*true*".
- "So far for Science — as much as we know of it." This line is the turn into the core argument.

**05 — Ever Crescendo** (L49–L61) **— the peak**

- Human nature "is the same now as it was a million of years ago". Then the escalation trap, built as one rising sequence.
- Each marvel must top the last ("ever *crescendo*… Test after test would be required"). The audience grows from "believers at Simla" to thousands, to "hundreds of millions" who can never be eyewitnesses.
- The stage cracks. "The ignorant… might some day vent their rage on the visible agents at work". Blavatsky stands alone on the stage, while "the higher and educated classes" tear "you to shreds". The tension breaks.
- Silence. Then the answer to Sinnett's complaint about secrecy: "the world's prejudices have to be conquered step by step, not at a rush."
- This is the scene the viewer should remember. It gets the largest span and the strongest visuals.

**06 — More Than One Martyr** (L62–L68)

- A slow walk down the gallery of martyrs. "As hoary antiquity had more than one Socrates so the dim Future will give birth to more than one martyr."
- Tableaux, in order:
  - Socrates
  - Aristarchus, "who 'affirmeth that the Earth moveth circularly about her own centre'"
  - Galileo before the Church, "a holocaust to the Bible"
  - Recorde starving in jail, while colleagues laugh at his *Castle of Knowledge*: "vain phantasies"
  - Gilbert, "Queen Elisabeth's physician", poisoned
  - Paracelsus, Agrippa and Dee, doubted
  - Bacon, at the end of the gallery, dismissing them all as "Alchemicians of the Fantastic philosophy"

**07 — The Natural Shields** (L70–L76)

- "All this is old history, you will think." A montage of the present day: a medium in an English dock, witch fires "in South America, Russia and the frontiers of Spain".
- The twist: "the charlatans and the jugglers are the natural shields of the 'adepts.'" A stage conjurer takes a bow. Behind him, unnoticed, a real adept passes.
- The adepts keep "the terrible weapons" secret for the public's own safety.
- Then the mood softens into the temple: phenomena are "reserved as a reward for those who have devoted their lives to serve the goddess Saraswati — our Aryan *Isis*." "Were they given to the profanes what would remain for our faithful ones?"

**08 — At Mr. Hume's** (L77–L79)

- "Many of your suggestions are highly reasonable and will be attended to."
- Flashback to Hume's drawing room. Hume argues brilliantly, and Sinnett agrees. A curtain stirs: K.H. "listened attentively to the conversation". We see the adepts' world, K.H. listening at a great distance, with Morya beyond him (optional).
- The verdict: Hume's "arguments are perfect from the standpoint of exoteric wisdom". There is a law neither man yet sees: causes the adepts can create, "but are as unable to control their consequences which thus become our masters".
- The scene ends on humility, the adepts admitting what even they cannot control.

**09 — Work With What You Have** (L81–L95)

- The letter turns practical, and Sinnett picks up a pen. Don't chase "new positions until those that you have already reached are secured". Publish "the note, the cup and the sundry experiments with the cigarette papers" together, because a single case "becomes less than worthless".
- Patience is heard off-screen. "Your and your lady's testimony" should not "go for nothing".
- Sir Donald Stewart: a man whose word the country accepts. "One witness of well known character outweighs the evidence of ten strangers", and in India that man "is — the Editor of the *Pioneer*."
- The closing parallel, at a dawn tomb. "One hysterical woman… at the pretended ascension", on whose word "countless milliards" have relied for "nearly 2,000 years". This is staged with care (decision D6).

**10 — Your Sincere Friend** (L97–L100)

- "Try — and first work upon the material you have and then we will be the first to help you to get further evidence."
- Cross-cut between two writers. K.H. writes the last lines and signs, "always your sincere friend, Koot' Hoomi Lal Singh", then folds the letter and sets down his pen. In Simla, Sinnett begins writing his article.
- Blavatsky in the doorway, a callback to scene 01: "They answered." "I said they would."
- The final image: the same Himalayan peaks through both windows, Simla's at sunset, K.H.'s under the stars.

**11 — Coda** ([context], analysis §9 and §11)

- Text cards on what happened next:
  - 1881: Sinnett publishes *The Occult World*.
  - 1884–85: the Coulomb affair and the Hodgson Report accuse Blavatsky of fraud. The educated classes did tear the movement "to shreds".
  - Who wrote the letters is still disputed.
- The final image is Blavatsky alone, then the letter on the desk.
- Self-contained, so it can be cut without changing scene 10.

## 7. Arcs

- **Sinnett:** eager certainty (01), then shock (02), unease (05), humility (08), resolve (09), and finally action (10). He enters wanting a public miracle, and leaves with a slower plan and his own name on the line.
- **Blavatsky:** a performer applauded (01), shown in danger (02, 05), unseen but sheltered (09), smiling for once (10), and alone in the coda (11). The letter is protective of her, and history wasn't.
- **K.H.:** a voice with no body (02) who becomes a presence (08) and finally a hand setting down a pen (10). We never fully see him in the same world as Sinnett.

## 8. Scene file format

Each `scene-NN.md` will contain:

1. **Header:** scene number and title, letter lines covered, settings, characters, feeling, and weight.
2. **Purpose:** what the scene must achieve in the story.
3. **Beat outline:** numbered beats before the full script.
4. **Script:** screenplay format in Markdown. Sluglines (`INT. SINNETT'S STUDY — NIGHT`), action lines, character cues, parentheticals and dialogue. K.H. lines are marked `(V.O.)` and tagged with the letter line, e.g. `[L13]`. Invented lines are marked `[dramatized]`.
5. **Visual and staging notes:** camera, light, colour, transitions, and which `images/anime/` file each character uses. These carry over to the website build.
