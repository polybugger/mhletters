# Mahatma Letters No. 1 — Analysis

Oct 22, 2026 · @Poly Bug

## 1. Summary

Koot' Hoomi (K.H.) writes to A. P. Sinnett and refuses his proposal for a decisive public test of the Masters' powers: making a newspaper appear far from where it was printed on its day of publication. The letter argues that the world is not ready for such proof, and that success would endanger Sinnett, "the devoted woman" and the cause. It backs this up with a long survey of how science and the Church have treated innovators. It ends with practical advice: publish the phenomena already witnessed at Simla and build the case step by step.

## 2. Source and setting of the text

- **Letter:** No. 1, the first letter in Section I, "THE OCCULT WORLD" SERIES, 1880–1881 **(editor)**.
- **Received-note:** "Received Simla about October 15th, 1880." The word "about" means the date is approximate **(editor)**.
- **Coverage:** this analysis covers the whole letter, from "Esteemed Brother and Friend" to the signature "Koot' Hoomi Lal Singh".
- **Chronological position:** the reading-order note gives only "Next: letter 2" and no previous letter, so this is the first letter in reading order **(editor)**. It answers a letter from Sinnett that is not included: "the present letter must satisfy you on many points you have better defined in your last" **(text)**. It also expects a further letter from him: "Your forthcoming letter I will not be able to fully answer…" **(text)**.
- **Transmission:** the text gives no notes on handwriting, copies or deletions, and has no footnotes.

## 3. Intended audience

- **Direct recipient:** Sinnett, addressed as "Esteemed Brother and Friend". He is identified as "the Editor of the *Pioneer*" **(text)**. The name Sinnett comes from the book's title and editorial framing, not from this letter **(editor)**.
- **Expected secondary reader:** Mr. Hume. K.H. comments on Hume's arguments and says "both you and he" will one day understand **(text)**. That suggests the writer expects Hume to hear of the letter **(inferred)**.
- **Wider audience:** the public Sinnett is urged to "instruct" through the *Pioneer* **(text)**, and in time readers of Sinnett's books **(context)**.
- **"You"** shifts. Mostly it means Sinnett personally ("yourself who originated the idea"). In places it widens to his "race", "your age" and "your science", meaning Victorian Europeans as a whole **(text)**.
- **"We" and "us"** means the writer's own brotherhood of adepts, keepers of a long-term "secrecy" **(text)**.

## 4. What prompted it

**What the letter reveals (text)**

- Sinnett proposed "the test of the London newspaper", which "would close the mouths of the skeptics".
- He argued that "half London would be converted if you could deliver them a *Pioneer* on its day of publication."
- He had been critical of the Masters' "great secrecy".
- A "conversation which took place at Mr. Hume's" had discussed these matters.
- Phenomena had already been produced at Simla: "the note, the cup and the sundry experiments with the cigarette papers", and "the brooch".
- An account of one case had appeared in the *Pioneer*: "the case under notice in the *Pioneer*".
- "The devoted woman" was being pressed for "newly devised and still more startling phenomena" and was "made to suffer" for those already produced.

**Background (context)**

- Helena Petrovna Blavatsky and Henry Steel Olcott stayed with the Sinnetts at Simla in the autumn of 1880.
- Several reported phenomena took place in early October, including a cup and saucer found buried at a picnic and a lost brooch recovered at the Humes' house.
- In *The Occult World* (1881), Sinnett describes asking for a copy of that day's London *Times* to appear at Simla. The letter frames the test in both directions ("the London newspaper"; "deliver them a *Pioneer*" in London). The exact form of the proposal is best checked against Sinnett's own account.
- Sinnett sent his first letter to an unknown "Brother" through Blavatsky shortly before this reply.

## 5. Main topics and argument

1. **The refusal.** The test is refused precisely because it would work: "Precisely because the test of the London newspaper would close the mouths of the skeptics — it is unthinkable."
2. **The world is unprepared.** Science could not explain the event, and "the ignorant masses" would see it as a miracle or as the work of "dark agencies". Every witness "would be thrown off his balance".
3. **Danger to Sinnett and "the devoted woman".** If people believed the event, "they would kill you before you could make the round of Hyde Park". If they did not, Sinnett would lose his "reputation and good name". For her, notoriety would be "a trap — and a fatal one indeed".
4. **Few are really interested.** Even among Sinnett's friends, "You could count them upon the fingers of your right hand."
5. **The free-thought boast is false.** The age claims to have bottled up "prejudice and religious bigotry" like "the wicked *Jin*". The writer doubts it: "Aye; but is it verily so?"
6. **Old knowledge before modern science.** Inquiry long predates the Royal Society ("*Roma ante Romulum fuit*"). Extinct races knew the *vril* or *Akás*, which modern science calls "an impossibility, a myth".
7. **Science admits only what fits its own methods.** Using Hooke as the example, the letter says modern scientists prefer a "convenient 'classification'" to deeper connections. For them a hypothesis need only be "*plausible*", not "*true*".
8. **Human nature does not change.** Prejudice, selfishness and pride are "the same now as it was a million of years ago", "especially of the middle and lower classes".
9. **The escalation trap.** Each success would demand a bigger one, "ever *crescendo*". Eyewitness proof cannot reach "the hundreds of millions".
10. **Danger to the "visible agents".** The ignorant might "vent their rage on the visible agents", while the educated "would go on disbelieving".
11. **A defence of secrecy.** Prejudice must be "conquered step by step, not at a rush". History is a record of persecuted innovators: Socrates, Aristarchus, Galileo, Recorde, Gilbert, Paracelsus, Agrippa and Dee.
12. **The same persecution today.** The letter points to the "recent persecutions of mediums in England" and witch-burnings. "The charlatans and the jugglers are the natural shields of the 'adepts'".
13. **Phenomena are a reward for the devoted.** They are reserved for those who "serve the goddess Saraswati — our Aryan *Isis*", not for "the profanes".
14. **Practical strategy.** Sinnett should secure "positions… already reached". He should publish the note, the cup and the cigarette papers together, because a single case is "positively injurious". He should trust his own standing as a witness, since "One witness of well known character outweighs the evidence of ten strangers."
15. **The single-witness parallel.** Christianity has rested "for nearly 2,000 years" on the word of "one hysterical woman" at "the pretended ascension". The implication is that one credible witness can carry a claim.

## 6. Concepts needed to understand it

| Concept | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| The newspaper test | Sinnett's proposed proof: a newspaper made to appear far from where it was printed on its day of publication, too quickly for any ordinary means |
| Phenomena | Paranormal effects reportedly produced by or through the Masters. Here the note, the cup, the cigarette papers and the brooch at Simla |
| Adepts / "we" | Members of a hidden brotherhood claimed to hold occult knowledge and powers. The letter puts "adepts" in quotation marks, as a term the public uses |
| *Akás* (Akasha) | Sanskrit *ākāśa*, "ether" or "space". Here, the subtle force or medium behind the phenomena, which the writer equates with Bulwer-Lytton's *vril* |
| *vril* | The all-powerful energy in Edward Bulwer-Lytton's novel *The Coming Race* (1871). The letter uses it as a familiar Western name for *Akás* and claims it was "the common property of races now extinct" |
| Natural, not supernatural | The claim that phenomena follow natural laws science has not yet found. It sets the Masters' position against both miracle-belief and materialist denial |
| Exoteric / esoteric | Outer, public knowledge versus hidden knowledge open only to initiates. Hume's arguments are "perfect from the standpoint of exoteric wisdom" |
| Causes and consequences | The adepts can create causes "at our will" but cannot control their consequences, "which thus become our masters". This idea is close to karma (inferred) |
| Saraswati, "our Aryan *Isis*" | The Hindu goddess of learning, paired with the Egyptian Isis. Isis was a Theosophical symbol of hidden wisdom, as in Blavatsky's *Isis Unveiled* (1877) (context). Here she stands for the devotion that earns phenomena |
| "Aryan" | In 19th-century usage, the Indo-European peoples and especially ancient India. The writer uses it for his own tradition. The word's later racial and political meanings postdate the letter |
| Visible agents vs invisible operators | The public figures who front the movement and take the backlash, versus the hidden Masters who actually work |
| Inductive philosophy | Bacon's method of reasoning from observed particulars to general laws, the foundation story of modern experimental science |
| The Invisible College and the Royal Society | A circle of 17th-century natural philosophers, and the scientific society chartered in London in 1662 (context) |
| *Roma ante Romulum fuit* | Latin proverb: "There was a Rome before Romulus." Every tradition has unseen predecessors |
| The Jin and the Solomons | From Arabian Nights–style folklore: Solomon seals a jinn in a bottle and casts it into the sea. Here bigotry is the jinn and scientists are the "Solomons" claiming to have sealed it away |
| Mediums and spiritualism | Victorian séance culture. Several mediums were prosecuted in England in the 1870s (context), and the letter treats them as fellow victims of prejudice |

## 7. Issues raised in the letter

These are the letter's own concerns, in its own terms.

- **Public unreadiness:** people would read a true phenomenon either as a miracle or as the work of "dark agencies".
- **The limits of science:** science cannot yet explain *Akás* and admits facts only through "their own modes of investigation".
- **Danger to Sinnett:** to his life if believed, and to his "reputation and good name" if not.
- **Danger to "the devoted woman":** notoriety is "a fatal" trap for her, and she is already "made to suffer".
- **The escalation trap:** each test demands a greater one, with no end.
- **Scale:** eyewitness proof cannot reach "hundreds of millions".
- **Hostility on both sides:** the ignorant may turn violent, and the educated will keep up their ridicule.
- **Sinnett's criticism of the Masters' secrecy:** the letter is answering it.
- **Misusing the phenomena:** an isolated case in the press "becomes less than worthless", so they should be published together.
- **The worth of testimony:** Sinnett and his wife's testimony should not "go for nothing" just because there were few witnesses.

**Era**

- **The British Raj.** India was ruled directly by the Crown after 1858. A small European elite governed a vast population. The letter's "hundreds of millions" who "could not be made eye-witnesses", and "the very territory belonging to you", reflect this **(context)**.
- **Victorian confidence in science.** The age's boast of having "liberated… the genius of knowledge, wisdom and freethought", and the letter's long argument with "your science", answer the prestige of Victorian science after Darwin and the rise of scientific naturalism **(context)**.
- **Spiritualism and its trials.** The "recent persecutions of mediums in England" refers to 1870s prosecutions of mediums **(context)**. Sinnett's assumption that a public test would convince reflects the period's demand that psychic claims be shown under test conditions.
- **Religious controversy.** Treating the resurrection witness as "hysterical" and "not over trustworthy" echoes 19th-century critical lives of Jesus, notably Renan's *Vie de Jésus* (1863) **(inferred)**.

**Setting**

Simla was the summer capital of British India, where the Viceroy and senior officials spent the season **(context)**. Its society was small and close-knit, and news spread fast. That explains why phenomena at a picnic or a dinner at Hume's could become public controversy, and why the writer weighs Sinnett's standing as "the Editor of the *Pioneer*" so carefully.

## 10. Fact check of the letter's own claims

| Claim (quote) | Verdict | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "1662, when Bacon, Robert Boyle and the Bishop of Chester transformed under the royal charter their 'Invisible College' into a Society" | Partly inaccurate | The Royal Society's first charter is 1662, and Boyle was a founder. Bacon died in 1626 and was its inspiration, not a founder. Wilkins was a founder but only became Bishop of Chester in 1668. Whether Boyle's "Invisible College" was the Royal Society's direct forerunner is disputed |
| The Royal Society arose "upon the plan of the 'Prophetic Scheme'" | Disputed | Possibly Bacon's *New Atlantis* (1627) and its ideal research college. The phrase itself is not identified |
| Archimedes was "the great Syracusian" | Accurate | Archimedes was from Syracuse in Sicily |
| *Vril* from the "Coming Race" | Accurate | *Vril* is the energy in Bulwer-Lytton's *The Coming Race* (1871), a novel. The claim that it was the "common property of races now extinct" is unverifiable |
| "a cave full of the skeletons of these giants" in the Himalaya | Unverifiable | No such find is known |
| In the "*Preface* to the 'Micrographia'", Hooke valued "external operation on the senses" over "intimate relations of objects" | Disputed | *Micrographia* (1665) has a long preface praising instruments that extend the senses. The letter's summary is one reading of it |
| Newton's discoveries "found in [Hooke] their greatest opponent" | Accurate | Hooke disputed Newton's theory of light (1672) and claimed priority on the inverse-square law |
| Aristarchus of Samos held that the Earth moves, long before Copernicus | Accurate | Aristarchus proposed a heliocentric model in the 3rd century BCE. The quoted phrase "affirmeth that the Earth moveth circularly about her own centre" is unsourced |
| "the Church sought to sacrifice Galileo" | Accurate in substance | Galileo was tried by the Inquisition in 1633 and lived under house arrest. He was not executed; "holocaust" is figurative |
| Robert Recorde was "the ablest mathematician at the court of Edward VI", "left to starve in jail by his colleagues" | Partly inaccurate | Recorde held royal posts under Edward VI. He died in 1558 in the King's Bench prison, held for debt after losing a libel suit brought by the Earl of Pembroke, not through his colleagues. *The Castle of Knowledge* (1556) is real. The "vain phantasies" quote is unsourced |
| Gilbert was "Queen Elisabeth's physician" and "died poisoned" | Partly inaccurate | Gilbert was Elizabeth I's physician. He died in 1603, probably of plague. No known evidence points to poisoning |
| Gilbert pointed out "Copernican's fallacy as to the 'third movement'" | Accurate | In *De Magnete* (1600) Gilbert argued that Copernicus's third motion, used to keep the Earth's axis parallel, was unnecessary |
| Gilbert was "this real founder of Experimental Science in England" | Disputed | A common judgement of Gilbert, but a matter of opinion |
| Bacon called such men "Alchemicians of the Fantastic philosophy" | Unverifiable | Bacon did criticise Paracelsus, and Gilbert's magnetic philosophy, but this exact phrase is not identified |
| Bacon was "Chancellor of England" and "Father of Inductive Philosophy" | Accurate | Lord Chancellor from 1618, Baron Verulam. "Father of inductive philosophy" is a standard label |
| "recent persecutions of mediums in England" | Accurate | Mediums such as Henry Slade (1876) were prosecuted in England, partly under the Vagrancy Act |
| "the burning of supposed witches, and sorcerers in South America, Russia and the frontiers of Spain" | Unverifiable | Press reports of witch-killings in rural Russia and elsewhere circulated in the 1870s. The specific cases meant are not identified |
| Human nature unchanged for "a million of years" | Unverifiable | A rhetorical claim. It also assumes a far longer human past than most Victorian readers accepted |
| "the hundreds of millions" who could not be eyewitnesses | Accurate | India's 1881 census counted about 250 million people |
| The resurrection rests on "one hysterical woman… at the pretended ascension" | Inaccurate | The Gospels give several women as witnesses of the empty tomb, and other witnesses to appearances. "Ascension" is the wrong event, since the Ascension is a different event from the resurrection appearances. The "hysterical woman" view echoes Renan |

## 11. Critical issues with the letter

- **Disputed authorship.** The letter is attributed to K.H. but reached Sinnett through Blavatsky **(context)**.
  - In 1885 the Hodgson Report for the Society for Psychical Research concluded that Blavatsky wrote the Mahatma letters, with help.
  - Theosophists rejected the finding. Later critics of the report, notably the handwriting examiner Vernon Harrison (1986), argued its method was flawed.
  - Authorship is still contested.
  - The historical errors in section 10 matter to this debate, though they don't settle it. Skeptics see writing typical of Blavatsky's reading. Defenders ask why an adept should be historically precise.
- **Unfalsifiable reasoning.** The letter refuses the test because it would succeed. A decisive proof is thus ruled out in advance, and the refusal can't be told apart from an inability to perform. The argument that "charlatans and jugglers" shield real adepts has the same structure: exposing fraud leaves the core claim untouched.
- **Class and colonial attitudes.**
  - The letter singles out "the middle and lower classes" for prejudice, and describes "the ignorant masses" as liable to riot.
  - Its grand phrases ("Your race", "our Aryan *Isis*") use 19th-century categories of race and civilisation.
  - It turns the colonial relationship around: the colonised East holds knowledge the ruling West lacks.
- **Treatment of Christianity.** Calling the resurrection "the pretended ascension" and its witness "hysterical" and "not over trustworthy" is deliberately provocative. It sits awkwardly beside the letter's complaint about others' prejudice.
- **Mixed message on publicity.** The letter warns against notoriety but tells Sinnett to publicise the phenomena already produced. The difference it draws is gradual versus sudden disclosure, which is a fine line.
- **Predictions.**
  - The warning that the educated classes would go on "tearing you to shreds" came true for Blavatsky in 1884–85.
  - The warning about Sinnett's "reputation and good name" partly did too: he lost the *Pioneer* editorship in 1883, reportedly in part because of his Theosophical activities **(context)**.
  - The fear of violence against the "visible agents" did not come true.

## 12. Open questions

- **Sir Donald Stewart:** why him in particular? Is the point his testimony on some public event, or his standing in general?
- **The test itself:** what exactly did Sinnett propose? The letter mentions both "the London newspaper" and delivering "a *Pioneer*" in London.
- **The *Pioneer* account:** which case was "under notice in the *Pioneer*", the brooch or another?
- **"the note":** which phenomenon is this, separate from the cup and the cigarette papers?
- **"The Heavenly White Virgin" (*Akás*):** what work or tradition does this refer to, and how is it linked to *De Magnete*?
- **The "Prophetic Scheme":** the source of the phrase.
- **The quotation "affirmeth that the Earth moveth circularly about her own centre":** which older English source is it from?
- **"those who generally deal with the European mystics":** who are they in the Masters' scheme?
- **"as you have been told":** where was Sinnett told earlier that the occult "weapons" became "deadly in the hands of the wicked and selfish"? Perhaps in conversation with Blavatsky.
